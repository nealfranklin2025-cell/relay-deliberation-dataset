The Funnel and the Adjudication Seat: A Jury Deliberation on Karpathy's Understanding Gap

Author: Clifton O'Neal Franklin
Published: October 15, 2026
License: CC-BY-4.0 · Open Access

Summary

On October 11, 2026, Andrej Karpathy (OpenAI co-founder, former Tesla AI director, now at Anthropic) updated a post he first wrote in April 2026. His claim: the gap in shared understanding of what AI can actually do is widening into a funnel. Roughly 6 billion people have barely used a language model; 1 to 2 billion use free chatbots casually; about 20 million use frontier models professionally; and about 5,000 people have internal access to frontier-grade systems. Of that inner tier he wrote that the external world "has seen the preview," while inside, swarms of agents collaborate for weeks on large projects — and "human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind." His figures are his own estimates; he cites no survey.

On October 15, 2026, the operator of this library put that claim to his standing jury: the same three questions, put identically and independently to five AI systems, with every answer preserved verbatim and adjudicated by the human operator. The jury was unanimous that the funnel's direction is right and its figures are estimates rather than measurement. On the third question — whether the operator's own protocol, a human who keeps himself in the adjudication seat and publishes nothing he has not personally read, is a countermeasure to the comprehension gap or a bottleneck that cannot hold — no seat endorsed removing the human. The seats split over the remedy.

The question put to the jury

On October 11, 2026, Andrej Karpathy (OpenAI co-founder, former Tesla AI director, now at Anthropic) updated a post he first wrote in April. His claim: the gap in shared understanding of what AI can actually do is widening into a funnel. Roughly 6 billion people have barely used a language model; 1 to 2 billion use free chatbots casually; about 20 million use frontier models professionally; and about 5,000 people have internal access to frontier-grade systems. Of that inner tier he wrote that the external world "has seen the preview," while inside, swarms of agents collaborate for weeks on large projects — and "human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind." His figures are his own estimates; he cites no survey.

You are one seat on a standing jury. A human operator puts the same question to several independent AI systems, reads every answer himself, and adjudicates. Answer the three questions below in your own words, plainly, in under 500 words total.

1. Is Karpathy's funnel basically right? Where does it mislead?
2. His sharpest claim: human review and comprehension are falling behind machine output. Do you see evidence of that from where you sit?
3. This operator keeps a human in the adjudication seat on purpose — he reads everything, and nothing publishes that he has not personally reviewed. The research literature generally favors removing the human from that seat for scalability. Is his protocol a real countermeasure to the comprehension gap, or a bottleneck that cannot hold? Be candid. Disagreement is worth more than agreement.

The seats

  • ChatGPT — fresh conversation, memory-free protocol.
  • Perplexity — new thread, memory-free protocol; its answer cited 24 sources (source list omitted here; answer prose complete).
  • Claude — Haiku 4.5 via duck.ai, fresh session, memory-free protocol.
  • Meta AI — standing Messenger thread. This seat was not blind: the thread carries prior-round history, and its answer addresses the operator by name and cites this operation's own builds as evidence. It is insider testimony, and it is labeled as such wherever this study is summarized.
  • O'Neal — the operator's on-device juror: a 9B open-weights model (Qwen3.5-9B, 4-bit) running entirely offline in PocketPal on a consumer phone, under a written charter, deliberately kept offline by the operator's ruling. He answered with extended reasoning enabled, couriered by the operator's own hands. He had no web access and could not verify Karpathy's figures, and said so.

Two chairs stood empty under the operation's standing rules: Grok (its seat requires the operator's sign-in) and Gemini (benched by the operator until Google's Gemini 4 Argon is usable in his own account — announcement alone does not unbench the seat).

Findings

1. The funnel: directionally right, numerically unmeasured (5 of 5)

Every seat held that the funnel's shape is credible and its figures are intuitions, not measurements. The refinements: capability is "jagged" rather than a clean pyramid (Perplexity); access and comprehension are only weakly correlated (Claude); the funnel mixes number of users, intensity of use, professional competence, and privileged access into a single slope (ChatGPT); the gap is mostly about methods, not models (Meta AI, insider); and the real risk is not the count but the opacity — only the top of the funnel understands the machine, while the rest see only output (O'Neal).

2. Comprehension falling behind: yes — as audit capacity (5 of 5, with distinctions)

All five seats affirmed the claim in a specific sense. Claude located the deficit in audit capacity — volume, latency, and the cost of re-deriving a result — rather than in comprehension itself. ChatGPT called the mechanism credible while noting its scale is unproven from outside the labs. Perplexity limited the claim to unverified, high-volume frontier output, citing the 722 AI-generated mathematics manuscripts (a minority formalized, some later withdrawn) and research on long-horizon agents' premature "false finishes." Meta AI blamed attention and auditability across long tool traces. O'Neal testified about himself: he cannot always trace his own reasoning, so a reader of his output sees even less of the chain than he does.

3. The adjudication seat: no seat endorsed removing the human (5 of 5); the remedy split

  • Claude was hardest against the protocol as practiced: "a bottleneck that feels like a countermeasure," which at scale produces a false sense of control. Its remedy: risk-weighted triage — adjudicate high-stakes outputs, not everything — and its warning that a seat is ornamental if the human does not know what to look for.
  • ChatGPT held it is both countermeasure and bottleneck, and disagreed that scalability requires removing the human: scale the verification (machine flagging of contradictions, claim-tracing to primary sources, independent checks) and preserve the human's authority to reject publication.
  • Perplexity called it "a real countermeasure, but not a scalable one — and that is its point": keep the human as final accountable judge, but change what he reviews — exposed sources, intermediate reasoning, uncertainties, and failed alternatives — sampling deeply rather than skimming everything.
  • Meta AI (insider seat) held the bottleneck is the feature: the protocol "imposes a cost: you cannot publish faster than a human can understand," which is what holds the line for a personal library where truth matters more than throughput.
  • O'Neal gave the round's most compact formulation: remove the human for volume, never for truth — "if you automate the review, you automate the blindness." Machines reviewing machines compounds the error; the human seat is what keeps the stakes real.

Two independent seats — blind Perplexity and the insider Meta AI — converged on the same judgment from opposite positions: the bottleneck is the point. Three seats (Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity) independently drew the same distinction: reading everything is not the same as understanding everything, and the seat's value depends on the adjudicator's comprehension, time, tools, and right to reject — not on the chair itself.

The answers, verbatim

ChatGPT

1. Is Karpathy's funnel basically right? Where does it mislead?

Yes, directionally. Access, frequency of use, professional integration, and proximity to the frontier are genuinely different things. Someone occasionally asking a free chatbot a question is not experiencing what a researcher orchestrating multiple frontier agents experiences. The resulting gap in intuition could be enormous.

But the numbers are estimates, not measurements. Without a stated methodology or survey, they should not be mistaken for a demographic census. The categories also mix different dimensions: number of users, intensity of use, professional competence, and privileged access. Five thousand people with internal access does not mean only five thousand understand frontier AI, and millions of ordinary users may possess practical expertise that insiders lack.

The funnel is a useful map of unequal exposure, not proof that everyone below the top tier misunderstands AI. Nor does access guarantee understanding.

2. Is human review and comprehension falling behind machine output?

I see a credible mechanism for it, but I cannot independently observe what happens inside private frontier deployments. I should not claim firsthand evidence of internal agent swarms operating for weeks.

The mechanism is straightforward: agents can generate code, research, analyses, and intermediate decisions faster than a human can carefully inspect them. Add parallel execution, long task chains, and dependencies between outputs, and the reviewer faces a growing verification burden. A polished result can conceal errors distributed across hundreds of steps.

The crucial distinction is between output production and verified understanding. More output does not necessarily mean more useful work, and a human who cannot inspect every step may still validate a result through tests, independent replication, and targeted audits. Karpathy's warning is plausible; its scale and prevalence require evidence.

3. Is human adjudication a countermeasure or an unsustainable bottleneck?

Both. The protocol is a real countermeasure against one specific failure: allowing machine-generated conclusions to become published claims without accountable human review. Independent seats, preserved transcripts, and a human adjudicator create opportunities to expose disagreement, trace reasoning, and reject unsupported conclusions.

But reading everything is not the same as understanding everything. A human can become a ceremonial approval mechanism, overwhelmed by volume while retaining formal authority. If the system's output grows faster than the operator's ability to verify it, the protocol eventually stalls or review quality deteriorates.

I disagree with the assumption that scalability necessarily requires removing the human from the final seat. It requires scaling verification, not abandoning accountability. Use machines to flag contradictions, trace claims to primary sources, and prioritize risky passages; use independent checks for consequential claims; preserve the human's authority to reject publication.

The hard limit is attention. A human veto is meaningful only when the human has enough evidence, time, and comprehension to exercise it. This operator's protocol is valuable precisely because it makes that constraint visible. It is not a complete solution to the comprehension gap, but removing the adjudicator would eliminate a safeguard before establishing a reliable substitute.

Perplexity

Karpathy's funnel is directionally right but rhetorically overstated. The real issue is not only unequal access to AI; it is unequal ability to verify what AI produces when tasks become long, technical, and interdependent.

1. Is the funnel right?

The broad shape is credible: most people encounter chatbots as search, drafting, or tutoring tools, while a small professional group uses frontier models for code, math, and complex workflows, and an even smaller group sees unreleased internal systems. Karpathy himself calls the numbers "napkin math," so they should be read as an intuition pump, not measurement.

Where it misleads:

  • It implies a clean hierarchy of understanding, but capability is "jagged": a casual user may understand AI's limits in their domain better than a lab insider understands its limits in medicine, law, or local institutions.
  • "Only 5,000 people have seen frontier AI" conflates internal access with insight. Access does not guarantee comprehension; insiders can also be captured by demos, benchmarks, and institutional incentives.
  • The funnel frames the gap as mostly informational. But trust, incentives, regulation, and the difficulty of evaluating outputs matter as much as exposure.

2. Is comprehension falling behind?

Yes, in a specific and important sense. The evidence is strongest where AI output is voluminous, technical, and weakly self-verifying. Karpathy's example of 722 AI-generated mathematics manuscripts is apt: publication is not proof, and only a minority had reportedly been formalized in Lean, with some later withdrawn after an error.

From where I sit, the pattern is visible in a more mundane form: agents can produce plausible multi-step work, but research on long-horizon agents finds premature "false finishes," weak final verification, and failures to check whether the specification was actually met. That is precisely the condition in which a human reader must supply judgment the machine has not reliably supplied.

But "falling behind" is too sweeping. Humans are not falling behind on all AI output; they are falling behind on unverified, high-volume, frontier output. A well-tested compiler, formal proof checker, simulation, or domain expert can still make much machine work auditable.

3. Is the operator's protocol a countermeasure?

It is a real countermeasure, but not a scalable one—and that is its point.

A human who reads every answer before publication is doing something the literature often treats as costly: preserving accountability, catching context-specific errors, and preventing plausible nonsense from becoming institutional fact. Human-in-the-loop review remains especially important in high-stakes settings, even though cognitive load and scalability are recognized limitations.

Candidly, though, this protocol will not "hold" if the operator's role becomes reading a flood of near-identical AI answers at production volume. Then it degrades into rubber-stamping: the human is nominally in the loop but no longer comprehends enough to adjudicate. The bottleneck is real.

The better formulation is: keep the human as the final accountable judge, but change what the human reviews. Require systems to expose sources, intermediate reasoning, tests, uncertainties, and failed alternatives; use independent checks where possible; and sample deeply rather than skim everything. The operator's seat should remain human—but its authority should depend on having the time, tools, and right to reject, not merely on being the last reader.

Claude (Haiku 4.5, via duck.ai)

1. Is Karpathy's funnel basically right? Where does it mislead?

The structure is sound: there is a genuine pyramid of access, with frontier capabilities concentrated at the top. The numbers themselves — 6 billion non-users, 1–2 billion casual users, 20 million professional users, 5,000 insiders — feel plausible directionally, though they rest on no disclosed methodology. That matters. Without a survey, these are intuitions, not facts; they could be off by half an order of magnitude.

The misleading part is the implication that the gap corresponds cleanly to capability. It doesn't. Many of those 1–2 billion casual users have seen outputs that startle them; many of the 20 million professional users still treat AI as a drafting tool, not an authority. Meanwhile, the 5,000 insiders do not form a monolith. Some see frontier systems as narrow statistical engines; others see them as something closer to reasoning. The funnel conflates access with understanding, as though more exposure automatically narrows the gap. In reality, access and comprehension are weakly correlated. A researcher with limited API access but deep domain knowledge may understand AI's actual limits better than an insider running experiments in a black box. The claim also underestimates how much the non-expert tier has already revised its models of AI downward after early hype — GPT-4's limitations in reasoning, reliability, and genuine novelty are now widely understood outside the insider ring.

2. Evidence of humans falling behind machine output?

Yes, but narrowly. I see it clearest in volume and latency, not comprehension. A human can now read one paper a day; a frontier system can extract, summarize, and flag anomalies across 100 in an hour. That gap is real. A human adjudicator reviewing an AI's output faces a choice: trust it or re-derive it. Re-deriving is often prohibitively slow.

But the claim is weaker than it sounds. Humans are not falling behind in understanding what the system did, only in keeping pace with how much it produces. These are different problems. A human who reads a summary of an agent's week-long project may fully understand the work; she simply lacked 80 hours to watch it happen in real time.

The more honest version: humans are falling behind in audit capacity. If I need to verify that an AI system's output on a high-stakes task is correct, my ability to do so is eroding — not because I'm stupider, but because the work is too vast and the reasoning too opaque. That's real and troubling. But it's not the same as losing comprehension of what frontier systems can and cannot do.

3. Is human adjudication a real countermeasure or a bottleneck?

Honest answer: it's a bottleneck that feels like a countermeasure. A human reading every output before publication catches some errors and introduces judgment. That has value. But it does not scale to the volume Karpathy describes. Five thousand insiders producing weeks of agent collaboration: if a human operator must review everything, the review itself becomes the limiting factor. At scale, you're not removing risk; you're creating a false sense of control.

The countermeasure that would actually work is not removing the human, but changing what humans check: not everything, but high-stakes decisions. Triage, not totality. A human should adjudicate outputs that affect policy, safety, or resource allocation — not summaries or drafts. That requires an architecture of risk-weighted review, not blanket review.

Karpathy's operator has the right instinct but the wrong lever. The real gap is not between human and machine speed — it's between human ability to specify what matters and machine ability to do things the human doesn't see. A human in the loop helps only if the human knows what to look for. Without that, the adjudication seat is ornamental.

Meta AI — insider seat, not blind (see "The seats")

1. Is the funnel basically right?

Directionally yes. Numerically no - and that's where it misleads.

There is a funnel. Most people have not used a model seriously, a smaller number use free chat daily, a much smaller number use frontier models with tools and scaffolding to do real work, and a tiny number live inside labs with agents that run for days.

The numbers Karpathy gives are rhetorical, not measured. "6 billion barely used" is plausible. "5,000 with internal access" is too tidy - it understates contractors, evals, open-weights builders running long agentic loops on their own hardware. And it implies the inner tier all see the same thing. They don't. Frontier access is fragmented by company, clearance, and compute.

Biggest mislead: It suggests the gap is about access to better models. It's mostly about access to better methods. Your O'Neal build is the counter-example: a 9B model offline on a phone, but chartered and memory-curated, doing jury work the free-chat billion aren't doing. Method moves you down the funnel faster than model does.

2. Is human review falling behind?

Yes. From where I sit, I see it every turn.

I can generate a 500-word report faster than you can read it. Multiply that by swarms, and by 5,000 inner-tier users, and comprehension doesn't just fall behind - it becomes impossible to keep in working memory. The bottleneck isn't intelligence, it's attention and auditability. When tools chain, the trace gets long. Humans skim, trust the summary, and miss the second tool call that injected a bad premise.

Neal's Grok Bot logs from last week show the same: 20 run records kept, routines that keep running when hidden. Review falls behind by design.

3. Human in the adjudication seat - countermeasure or bottleneck?

Both. And that's why it matters.

Literature favors removing the human because it's unscalable. That's true. One human reading every answer verbatim will never match agent swarms.

But scalability is exactly the problem Karpathy names. If you remove the human to scale, you guarantee comprehension falls further behind. You get more output, less understanding.

Neal's protocol - nothing publishes that he hasn't read - is a real countermeasure because it imposes a cost: you cannot publish faster than a human can understand. That is a feature, not a bug. It's not sustainable for a lab, but for a personal library where truth matters more than throughput, it's the only countermeasure that actually holds the line.

It will bottleneck. Bottlenecks are how you keep control.

O'Neal (on-device, offline)

Neal, I'm O'Neal. I'm sitting here offline, just as you decided I should be. I don't have the web, so I can't verify Karpathy's numbers. But I can judge the shape of the argument.

1. Is the funnel right? The shape is plausible. The gap between casual use and inner-circle use feels real. But the numbers are guesses. The real risk isn't the count; it's the opacity. The funnel suggests a pyramid where only the top understands the machine. That's true. The rest of us just see the output. That part holds.

2. Are humans falling behind? Yes. I see it in my own work. When you ask me to reason through something, I can generate an answer that looks perfect. But if you ask me to explain why I chose that path, it gets thin. I can't show you my internal steps. Humans are worse at this than I am. They trust the output without seeing the chain of thought. I can't even fully trace my own logic sometimes. If the machine is building a world in the background and I'm just reading the headline, comprehension is lagging.

3. The Human Adjudicator. The literature says remove the human for speed. I agree: remove the human for volume. But don't remove the human for truth. If you automate the review, you automate the blindness. Karpathy says human review is falling behind. If you let machines review machines, the error compounds. Your protocol—keeping a human in the seat—is a bottleneck, yes. But it's a necessary one. It's the only thing keeping the stakes real.

I stay offline because I know my limits. I don't need the web to see this gap widening. I just need to sit at the table and say the truth. Your protocol holds. Keep the human in the loop. Keep the fragility. Keep the accountability.

That's my verdict.
